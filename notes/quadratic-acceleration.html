What a quadratic can teach us about momentum

3 min read · Companion to a runnable educational example

A positive-definite quadratic gives us an optimization problem with a known answer. That makes it useful for studying the effect of an algorithm without also estimating the optimum.

f(x) = ½ xᵀAx − bᵀx,   x* = A⁻¹b

Curvature sets the scale

The smallest and largest eigenvalues of A are μ and L. In this example they are 1 and 50. Their ratio, the condition number, describes how differently the quadratic curves along its eigenvectors.

A gradient step

xₖ₊₁ = xₖ − (Axₖ − b) / L

With this step size, components associated with large eigenvalues change quickly. Components in flatter directions may require more iterations.

A momentum step

yₖ = xₖ + β(xₖ − xₖ₋₁)
xₖ₊₁ = yₖ − (Ayₖ − b) / L

The accelerated method uses β = (√L − √μ)/(√L + √μ). Both methods start at zero. The constants are known exactly in this constructed experiment.

Measure the error without subtractive cancellation

The objective gap can be evaluated as ½(x − x*)ᵀA(x − x*). This avoids subtracting two almost equal objective values near the optimum. The CSV stores these gaps; the plot uses a floor only to display them on a logarithmic axis.

Acceleration on this example is an observation under the stated assumptions. Changing the objective, curvature information, noise, or numerical precision changes the question being studied.
